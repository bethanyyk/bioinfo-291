ABSTRACT
While many animals display behaviours resembling social conventions when interacting with conspecifics, it is 
unknown whether animals who live in close contact with humans develop sensitivity to our social conventions. 
With an eye-tracking study (N = 39), we asked whether dogs' (Canis familiaris) eye movements and pupil sizes 
would show a reaction when observing a violation in the customary response to human conventional commu￾nicative gestures (“come here” and “go away”). In the video stimuli, a human actress either approached or 
walked away from a second human actress. Both these actions (approaching or walking away) could be expected 
or surprising (within-subject), depending on whether they happened in response to the “come here” or the “go 
away” gesture of the second actress. While dogs' looking times and number of gaze transitions between the two 
actresses did not differ significantly between congruent and incongruent responses, their pupil size changes did: 
dogs' pupils dilated more when watching the incongruent responses (approaching after the “go away” gesture 
and walking away after the “come here” gesture) compared to the congruent ones. We discuss the possibility that 
pet dogs, during their ontogeny, implicitly form expectations about the usual response to some conventional 
human gestures. These expectations are not self-centred, as they were evidenced when dogs observed a third￾party interaction between two humans, and they are context-specific, as the same responses elicited surprise 
following one gesture but not the other. We also consider alternative explanations and limitations of our findings.
Social conventions, descriptive regularities about the usual behav￾iour in specific circumstances, are pervasive in human societies (Heyes, 
2024; Schmidt & Rakoczy, 2023). In particular, some intransitive ges￾tures in some cultures are well understood as communicative conven￾tions. For example, if we observe someone raise a hand and wave at 
another person, we immediately and effortlessly infer the intention to 
“greet”. Human expectations about the conventions in third-party social 
interactions begin to develop during the second half of the first year of 
life (Beier & Spelke, 2012). For example, 7- to 12-month olds have ex￾pectations about how members of the same social group should act 
(Powell & Spelke, 2013, 2018) and 11-month olds' pupil size changes 
show surprise when conformity does not lead to social inclusion and 
when non-conformity leads to social inclusion (Koster ¨ & Hepach, 2024). 
By their first year, human infants already expect language and artifacts 
to be used in a conventional way (Schmidt & Rakoczy, 2023). However, 
we still do not know the extent to which the ability to detect violations of 
social conventions, from the observation of non-verbal third-party 
interactions, is shared with other animals.
Dogs (Canis familiaris) are thought to follow some forms of social 
conventions during social play (Bekoff, 2015) and constitute a promising 
model for the study of the development and evolution of social 
convention understanding. Specifically, they have been proposed as a 
model for the study of socio-cognitive processes that might be func￾tionally analogous to those of humans as a result of convergent evolu￾tion, due to our shared social environment (Hare & Tomasello, 2005; 
Kaminski & Marshall-Pescini, 2014; Top´
al, Miklosi, ´ et al., 2009). For 
example, dogs are excellent readers of human non-verbal communica￾tion, to which they often respond appropriately (for reviews, see Huber, 
2016; Huber & Lonardo, 2023; Kaminski & Nitzschner, 2013). This in￾cludes an understanding of pointing gestures both as referential (Volter ¨
et al., 2025) and as indicating a location (Lakatos et al., 2012; Tauzin 
et al., 2015). However, dogs do not follow all types of pointing gestures 
equally well: they have difficulty with gestures which are less visually 
clear or less commonly encountered, such as elbow cross-pointing,

human hand gestures (commands) with actions. Studies with rescue

2016). Moreover, once learned the gestural signals (e.g., for “sit”, 
“down”, “stay” and “come”), rescue dogs respond to them irrespective of 
whether the gesture is emitted by their owner or a stranger (Scandurra

children, dogs did not seem to follow a human experimenter's deictic 
gesture when this was directed to another human (Kaminski et al., 2012) 
nor did they evaluate a human experimenter based on her interaction 
with another dog (Nitzschner et al., 2012). Hence, it is not known 
whether dogs have expectations about the usual responses to human 
non-verbal conventional gestures, when they observe interacting 
humans.
To investigate dogs' expectations about third-party non-verbal 
communicative gestures, we showed them video recordings of social 
interactions between two human actresses, one of which (the signaller) 
communicated to the other (the receiver) to approach her or to move 
away from her (Fig. 1). For the “come here” signal the signaller showed a 
series of conventional gestures associated with the intention to invite the 
receiver to approach (Guidetti & Nicoladis, 2008). These gestures are

In a 2 × 2 within-subject design, we varied the sender's signal (“come 
here” or “go away”) and whether the recipient's behavioural response 
was congruent or incongruent with the sender's signal. Following the 
“come here” signal, the congruent response was approaching the 
signaller, while the incongruent response was walking away from the 
signaller. In the “go away” condition, the congruent response was 
walking away, while the incongruent response was approaching. We 
hypothesised that, due to the experience they gain during their ontogeny 
in a human household (including their training), dogs acquired expec￾tations about the customary behaviour the receiver will adopt in 
response to the two communicative cues. Hence, we predicted that 
seeing the congruent response in both conditions should result in shorter 
looking times to the actresses as compared to seeing the incongruent 
response, which should instead violate dogs' expectations and result in 
longer looking times when dogs witness the incongruent reactions. 
Importantly, because the same behaviour (approaching or walking 
away) is in one condition the customary (expected) reaction and in the 
other condition the incongruent (surprising) reaction, this design allows 
to test whether the observers' expectations are context-relative, a 
signature of human understanding of conventions (Rakoczy & Schmidt, 
2013). Assuming that human communicative gestures can be considered 
social conventions (Lewis, 1969; Tomasello, 2007), we analysed the 
participants' pupil sizes as a measure of an orienting response and 
arousal elicited by a violation of a social convention. Our prediction was 
that their pupils would dilate more after observing the receiving actress's 
gesture-incongruent reaction than after observing a gesture-congruent 
reaction.
1. Methods
This experiment was discussed and approved by the ethics commit￾tee of the University of Veterinary Medicine of Vienna (ETK-195/12/ 
2022; ETK-53/03/2023). Data were collected using an Eyelink 1000 
eye-tracker (SR Research, Canada; sampling rate: 1000 Hz, monocular 
tracking of the right eye). Before taking part in the experiments, dogs 
underwent training (Karl et al., 2020) to lay their head on a chinrest 
(height adjusted depending on the dog's size), 70 cm away from the 
screen. Videos were presented on a 24-in. LCD monitor with resolution 
1024 × 768 px and refresh rate 100 Hz, to accommodate dogs' flicker￾fusion threshold (Coile et al., 1989). The criterion for successful 
training completion, and start of the tests, was that dogs performed a 
validation of a calibration with less than 1◦ of visual angle deviation and 
were able to maintain their head on the chinrest (without leaving when 
the trainer left their field of view) for up to one minute.
On testing days, each session began with a five-point calibration of 
each dog's gaze positions on the screen. We used animations (inside 
squares with a side length of 32 or 64 px) to attract the dogs' attention to 
each calibration point. During tests, the dog's trainer was never visible to 
the dogs. The sample size was determined by the number of chinrest￾and calibration-trained dogs available at the time of data collection. The 
data and code for statistical analyses of this experiment are available at: 
https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17523757 (Lonardo et al., 2026).
1.1. Stimuli
The familiarisation video showed two actresses throwing a Frisbee 
back and forth between each other for ten times (total duration of the 
video: 10 s), a scene that has been found to capture and hold dogs' 
attention (Volter ¨ et al., 2020). The agents were seen from their side and 
were facing each other, standing (Fig. 1a). This sequence was followed 
by an attention getter and only when the dogs fixated on it for 200 ms, 
was one test video presented. We decided to present the same famil￾iarisation video before each test video. The familiarisation introduced 
the scene and interacting actresses that would have also appeared in the 
test, but there was no obvious communicative signal yet. We decided on 
this design because we wanted to test the expectations the dogs bring to 
the lab due to their previous life experience with humans, rather than 
building expectations during the experiment with habituation trials 
(Sirois, 2022).
In test videos, the same two actresses and set-up as in the familiar￾isation were shown (Fig. 1a). The agent on the left side of the screen 
either gestured for the other agent to approach, for example by patting 
her hands on her thighs and beckoning (Fig. 1b) or to move away, by 
momentarily pointing in the other agent's direction (Fig. 1c). The non￾verbal communication took approx. 3 s and was followed by the reac￾tion of the agent on the right side of the screen, which could be either 
congruent with the communicated gesture (approaching when invited to 
do so or moving away when invited to do so) or incongruent with it 
(approaching when invited to move away or moving away when invited 
to approach). The reaction of the agent on the right side of the screen 
took approx. 3 s (Fig. 1d-e). Hence, we had two congruent conditions 
(come-approach: CC, go away-go away: GG) and two incongruent con￾ditions (come-go away: CG, go away-approach, GC). The videos were 
edited so as to show exactly the same footage of the communicative cues 
between the two conditions that shared the same communicative cues 
(same left half of the video) and exactly the same footage of the reaction 
(same right half of the video) between the two conditions that shared the 
same reaction. This was crucial to ensure that brightness was identical 
between these conditions and that the same reaction video was supposed 
to be surprising in one case but not in the other.
1.2. Subjects
We tested 39 dogs (18 females) of different breeds and mixes, mostly 
mesocephalic (Table S1 reports more demographic and counter￾balancing information). Their average age was 59.3 months. Three 
sessions, from three different dogs, were repeated due to the dogs 
leaving the chinrest before the reacting agent started to move. Two dogs 
became unavailable before the end of the data collection (one dog had 3 
missing sessions – CC, CG, GC; the other dog had 1 missing session - GC). 
Nine further dogs had trials in which their gaze was detected in less than 
70% of the relevant interest period (see below). To ensure a within￾subject comparison, we only retained dogs with a full dataset (all four 
valid trials, one per condition) in the pupil size analysis (N = 28).
1.3. Design
Dogs were tested on one test trial for each of the four conditions, 
since we were interested in comparing the looking times between the 
two incongruent and the two congruent trials. On each of four sessions a 
dog would see the familiarisation video (10 s, including 1 s at the 
beginning where the video is frozen) and one of the test videos (19 s, 
including 2 s at the beginning and 10 s at the end where the video is 
frozen), separated by an attention getter. Consecutive sessions were 
separated by at least one week.
We counterbalanced across dogs whether the first presented condi￾tion shows a congruent or an incongruent action. We pseudo￾randomised the order of presentation of the 4 conditions with the 
constraint that the 4 conditions are equally likely to be presented first

and the two conditions alternated from trial to trial (i.e., CICI or ICIC). 
We chose this option, instead of a blocked order of conditions, because it 
might minimise the risk that dogs build expectations about the second 
agent (e.g., “she always obeys/disobeys”). The design was balanced 
across dogs with regards to age and sex.
1.4. Analyses
Data were extracted using in DataViewer (version 5.1.1). All analyses 
were performed in R (R Core Team, 2025; version 4.2.2). The analyses of 
dwell times and gaze transitions between the agents' AoIs focused on an 
interest period (IP) spanning the last 13,200 ms of the test videos, from 
the moment the agent on the right started reacting (approaching or 
walking away).
1.4.1. Dwell times
We analysed the proportion of time the dogs spent making fixations 
(EyeLink parsing algorithm) in the agents' AoIs (relative to the time they 
spent looking at the screen during the whole IP), using GLMMs with beta 
error structure. We included as sole test predictor the interaction be￾tween trial type (congruent vs incongruent) or condition (CC, CG, GC, 
GG), in two separate models, and agent type (communicating vs react￾ing) as well as their fixed effects.
Both models included the fixed effect of trial number (z-transformed) 
as control predictor and the random slope of the test predictor (manually 
dummy coded and centered) and trial number (z-transformed) within 
the random intercept of subject ID. We scaled the dependent variables to 
ensure they did not contain the exact values of 0 and 1 (Smithson & 
Verkuilen, 2006).
1.4.2. Transitions between agents' AoIs
To analyse the effect of trial type on the number of gaze transitions 
dogs exhibited between the two agents' AoIs, we used a Generalized 
LinearMixed Model (GLMM; (Baayen, 2008) with Poisson error struc￾ture and loglink function (McCullagh & Nelder, 1989). We included as 
sole test predictor trial type (congruent vs incongruent) and trial number 
(z-transformed) as control predictor (both with fixed effect). To account 
for repeated observations from the same subjects, we included the 
random intercept of subject ID and the random slope of trial number (z￾transformed) and trial type (manually dummy coded and centered) to 
allow for inter-individual variation in the effect of these predictors on 
the number of transitions. Moreover, we included in the model an offset 
term (McCullagh & Nelder, 1989) to control for the duration of looking 
time (log transformed, base e) during each trial, since more transitions 
between AoIs are possible when dogs look longer to a trial.
1.4.3. Pupil size
We considered as baseline period the 1000 ms of test videos between 
the end of the signalling actress's gesture and the beginning of the 
movement of the reacting actress (between seconds 5 and 6 of the 
videos). Four trials were excluded from these analyses because the dogs' 
pupil was detected for less than 70% of the whole IP (which lasted 
13,900 ms, from the beginning of the baseline until the end of the video). 
Eight additional trials were excluded because, even though the eye was 
tracked correctly, the dogs' gaze was detected on-screen for less than 
70% of the whole IP, suggesting that dogs were keeping their position on 
the chinrest but probably not paying attention to the stimuli in those 
trials. Even when including these 12 trials, dogs' eyes were detected for 
95% of the time, averaging across dogs and trials, and their gaze was 
detected as on-screen for 92% of the time. To ensure a within-subject 
comparison, we only retained dogs with four valid test trials, one per 
condition (N = 28). Pupil size was analysed during a 4-s IP immediately 
following the beginning of the reaction (approach or walk away) of the 
actress who had witnessed the communicative gesture (from s 6 to s 10). 
We chose the 70% valid data cut-off and the 4-s measuring IP for con￾sistency with our previous eye-tracking studies with dogs (e.g., Lonardo 
et al., 2024).
The raw pupil size data were pre-processed following standard 
guidelines in the field (Kret & Sjak-Shie, 2019; Mathot ˆ et al., 2018). 
Specifically, pupil and gaze samples from the 100 ms preceding and 
following each blink were removed to decrease the likelihood of artifacts 
in the signal. We subsequently filled the resulting gaps of up to 500 ms 
using a linear interpolation. Next, we calculated the median speed of 
pupil diameter changes for each trial. We excluded samples associated to 
speed dilation outliers, defined as speed values exceeding eight times the 
median absolute deviation (MAD; Leys et al., 2013) and repeated the 
500-ms linear interpolation. We then subtracted the median pupil size 
during the baseline period from the interpolated data for each dog and 
trial. Finally, we downsampled the pupillometry and gaze coordinates 
data to 10 Hz, by retaining the median value for each time bin, to reduce 
autocorrelation in the signal, potentially deriving from the relatively 
slow pupil dilation response compared to the high original (1000 Hz) 
sampling rate. Finally, we repeated the MAD-based artefact correction 
and interpolation.
To the pre-processed and down-sampled data, we fitted a generalized 
additive mixed model (GAMM) with Gaussian error structure, using the 
bam function of the R package mgcv (version 1.8.41; Wood, 2011), with 
the smoothing parameter selection method set to “ML” (van Rij et al., 
2019). We included a linear term for trial type (congruent or incon￾gruent reaction), our test predictor, and one for session number (1 to 4), 
a control predictor. We included a smooth term for time and for the 
different levels of trial type over time (with the maximum number of 
basis functions set to 20). This allows us to model the different effects of 
time depending on the level of trial type, since we expected the effect of 
time on pupil size to be non-linear but not necessarily identical between 
the levels of trial type. We additionally included a smooth term for the 
interaction between the X- and Y-gaze coordinates, as the gaze position 
on the screen might influence pupil size (Mathot ˆ et al., 2018). Finally, 
we included random factor smooths term for subject and for each 
recording over time. To evaluate the significance of trial type, we 
visually inspected the estimated differences between congruent and 
incongruent trials using the function plot_diff of package itsadug. We 
assessed autocorrelation in the GAMMs by visually inspecting the cor￾relations between the residuals and the lagged residuals (function acf of 
the package stats; R Core Team, 2025). We checked the normality and 
homogeneity of the distribution of the residuals by inspecting a QQ-plot 
and a plot of residuals against fitted values (function gam.check, pack￾age mgcv). These checks confirmed that the autocorrelation was not 
excessive and that the assumptions of the models were met.
2. Results
2.1. Dwell times
Dogs looked at the agents on average for 4401 (SD ± 3668) ms (CC) 
and 3967 (SD ± 3296) ms (GG) in the congruent conditions and for 3939 
(SD ± 3549) ms (CG) and 4390 (SD ± 3464) ms (GC) in the incongruent 
ones. A beta-distributed GLMM fitted on the proportion dwell time 
revealed no significant interaction between trial type and agent type (χ2 
= 0.05, df = 1, p = .818). A reduced model without the interaction term, 
provided no evidence for a significant difference in dwell times between 
the congruent or incongruent trial type (χ2 = 0.12, df = 1, p = .731; 
Table S2 and Fig. 2a). Dogs looked significantly longer at the reacting 
than the communicating agent (average reacting agent: 5105 ± 1924 
ms, average communicating agent: 3218 ± 2065 ms; fixed effect of 
agent type: χ2 = 13.85, df = 1, p < .001; Table S2), consistently with the 
fact that the reacting agent was the only one moving during the IP 
(Fig. 3).
A second beta GLMM with condition (CC, CG, GC, GG) instead of trial 
type (congruent, incongruent) as test predictor confirmed that dogs' 
proportion of dwell time to the agents did not significantly depend on 
condition either nor on the interaction between condition and agent

2.2. Transitions between agents' AoIs
On average dogs exhibited 2.22 (SD ± 1.98; range 0–8) transitions 
between the two agents' AoIs per trial. Gaze transitions between the two 
agents did not differ significantly depending on the trial type (χ2 < 0.01, 
df = 1, p = .951; Table S3 and Fig. 2b). With increasing session number, 
dogs tended to exhibit less gaze transitions between the two agents (χ2 
= 2.95, df = 1, p = .086).

2.3. Pupil size
Dogs' pupil size was significantly larger when dogs witnessed the 
incongruent than the congruent reactions to the communicative gestures 
(linear effect of trial type: t = 2.04, p = .041). In particular, this dif￾ference between incongruent and congruent trials was significant be￾tween ms 7697 and ms 9111 (red line in Fig. 4B), i.e., approx. 1.8 s after 
the reacting actress started to move. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test of the 
dogs' mean pupil size in the same interest period confirmed that 
incongruent trials elicited larger pupil sizes than congruent trials (T+ =
313, N = 28, p = .011). Session number did not affect pupil size linearly 
(t = − 0.01, p = .990). The non-parametric effect of time (F = 11.87, p <
.001), gaze coordinates on the screen (F = 31.14, p < .001), subject over 
time (F = 0.09, p < .001) and the time series of each recording (F =
90.57, p < .001) significantly contributed to explaining the pupil size. 
The results of the GAMM analysing pupil size (N = 28) are shown in 
Table S4 and its partial effects are plotted in Fig. S2. When retaining all 
data for the analyses (N = 39), i.e., including the 11 dogs who did not 
provide valid data in each of the 4 test trials and including the trials in 
which dogs' gaze was detected and on-screen for less than 70% of the 
time, the parametric effect of trial type was not significant (t = 0.54, p =
.592). The non-parametric effect of time, gaze coordinates on the screen, 
and the combination of subject, condition and session number still 
significantly contributed to explaining the pupil size (Table S5; Fig. S3 
for partial effects plot).
3. Discussion
The aim of this eye-tracking study was to probe dogs' expectations 
about human social conventions, using eye tracking and pupillometry. 
Dogs' pupil size changes revealed their sensitivity to human conven￾tional gestures, specifically to the typical reaction to some human 
communicative gestures. We found this difference even though dogs' 
dwell times and number of gaze alternations between the two agents did 
not differ significantly.
Dogs paid close attention to the stimuli. On average, their gaze was 
detected by the eye-tracker and on-screen for 92% of the time and they 
alternated their gaze between the two agents' AoIs, suggesting that they 
recognized the socially relevant parts of the scenes, consistently with the 
findings of a previous eye-tracking study with dogs (Tornqvist ¨ et al., 
2020). In particular, their vertical gaze coordinates (Fig. S1) suggest that 
dogs focused their attention on the agents' faces and their dwell times as 
well as their horizontal gaze coordinates (Fig. 3) indicate that they 
closely followed the reacting agent's movements.
Importantly, our design can rule out that dogs' pupillary response is 
due to low-level (perceptual) features of the stimuli (including lumi￾nosity) because each video showing the reaction of the (approaching or 
walking away) agent served, in one condition, as the surprising 
(incongruent) stimulus and, in the other condition, as the expected 
(congruent) stimulus relative to the previous communicative gesture. 
This finding suggests that dogs might have third-party expectations 
about the appropriate response to human conventional gestural 
communication. Further, the pupillometry finding suggests that these 
expectations about what the agent will do are context-specific: the same 
action is considered in one case surprising and in the other case ex￾pected. There is evidence of animals (mainly apes) being sensitive to 
conventions in relation to the self, yet so far expectations about con￾ventions that do not directly involve the observer have not been re￾ported in non-human animals (Schmidt & Rakoczy, 2023). When 
addressed directly and ostensively, dogs are skilled at following human 
deictic gestures to locate hidden food (Kaminski & Nitzschner, 2013; 
ManyDogs Project, et al., 2023). In the present study, we found indirect 
evidence that, in the context of a third-party interaction between two 
humans, dogs expected a pointing gesture away from the communicator 
and in the direction of the recipient of the signal, to lead the recipient to 
move in the signalled direction. They also expected that the communi￾cator tapping with her hands on her thighs and beckoning would lead to 
the recipient to move closer. Dogs' pupil sizes reacted to the incongruent 
actions despite the dogs never being addressed by the depicted agents. 
Hence, dogs' expectations in this study were not self-centred and 
extended to third-party interactions between two humans.
Conventions, defined as behavioural regularities exhibited by a 
majority in recurring social circumstances (Lewis, 1969), allow ob￾servers to form expectations about how other individuals will typically 
behave in such circumstances. Importantly, however, conventions are 
different from norms in that they do not inform about how individuals 
ought to behave in a given context (Bicchieri, 2006). We argue that the 
present findings highlight dogs' sensitivity to human social conventions, 
i.e., dogs' expectations about the typical reaction an individual will 
show, but more research is needed to assess whether dogs might also 
understand social norms, intended as an understanding of how an in￾dividual ought to behave in a certain social situation. Interestingly, 
however, scholars approaching norm psychology from a comparative 
perspective have criticized heavily mentalistic accounts of norm psy￾chology, such as the one just described, and extend at least simpler 
notions of social norms also to non-human species (Andrews et al., 2024;

Westra et al., 2024). In this light, a few psychological processes are likely 
to give rise to normative regularities across species, and the present 
findings could indicate that dogs have at least one such process: a 
“submentalistic behaviour understanding” (Andrews et al., 2024), i.e., 
in this case, dogs formed expectations about the future reaction of the 
receiver of communicative gestures based on the dogs' own previous 
learning experience (associative learning through direct reinforcement 
or observational learning). Associative and reinforcement learning are 
also considered building blocks of human norm psychology (Andrews 
et al., 2024). Based on the present findings, we do not claim that dogs in 
their own behaviour follow human-like conventions nor that their ex￾pectations are necessarily homologous to those that humans might form 
watching the same stimuli. What our findings suggest is that dogs can 
detect violations of behavioural regularities when observing incon￾gruent human social interactions and thus dogs' expectations about 
some human communicative conventions might be functionally similar 
to those of humans.
Showing non-conspecific actors might make processing social in￾teractions more demanding for dogs (Tornqvist ¨ et al., 2015). However, 
we deliberately chose to show videos of human agents to investigate if 
domestic animals with extensive exposure to human social conventions 
would come to have expectations about some human-typical conven￾tions, and the pupillometry results provide initial evidence in favour of 
this hypothesis.
The dwell times confirmed that dogs paid attention to the human 
agents producing and reacting to human-typical communicative ges￾tures, even if the agents never addressed the dogs directly. Yet, dogs 
looked at the reacting actress equally long irrespective of whether her 
reaction was congruent or incongruent with the communicative gesture 
she had previously received.
Dogs' gaze pattern suggests that they were following the actions of 
the two humans in all videos to a similar extent. Indeed, on average they 
looked for about 4.4. s at the approaching actress, (irrespective of 
whether her approach was preceded by an invite to approach or by a 
signal to move away from the signalling actress) and for about 3.9/4.0 s 
at the agent moving away (irrespective of whether this was preceded by 
an invite or a signal to move away). This suggests that strictly matching 
the stimuli across conditions and controlling for low-level visual features 
of the stimuli ensuring comparable visual engagement across conditions 
is an important methodological concern for pupillometry studies with 
dogs.
One might wonder why dogs' dwell times and gaze alternations did 
not significantly differentiate between congruent and incongruent re￾sponses, while their pupil size changes did. First, the dogs' chin-rest 
training may have led to a ceiling effect in their dwell time data. 
Similar to adult human participants in eye-tracking studies, most of the 
dogs in our study stayed on the chin-rest and looked on the screen 
throughout the test trials, which might have reduced the variance in 
dwell times we might have observed otherwise. Second, eye movements 
(including measures such as dwell times and AoI transitions) may be less 
sensitive measures of expectancy violations than pupil dilation. This 
hypothesis is consistent with findings from developmental psychology 
with human infants and toddlers (Geangu et al., 2011; Hepach & 
Westermann, 2013; Sirois, 2009), as well as with a previous study with 
dogs (Lonardo et al., 2024). However, given the lack of effects on dogs' 
looking patterns, the pupillometry results need to be replicated and dogs' 
understanding of social expectations warrants further investigation, 
especially given that in this study we only probed dogs' sensitivity to the 
social conventions behind two communicative gestures.
Our study does not reveal when and how dogs acquire their expec￾tations regarding the common reactions to human conventional ges￾tures. For example, these expectations might originate from explicit 
training or mere exposure to human communicative gestures (observa￾tional learning). Either of these learning mechanism could result in ex￾pectations concerning the likely (i.e., more frequently co-occurring) 
reactions to the gestures presented in our stimuli. Our study shows, 
however, that these expectations extend to third-party interactions be￾tween two humans presented on a screen. Therefore, the dogs either 
acquired these expectations through first-person interactions with 
humans and transferred them to a third-person (human-human) context, 
or they acquired these expectations by observing human or dog-human 
interactions. Both possibilities would highlight how flexibly dogs keep 
track of the communicative interactions in their social environment and 
thus our findings pave the way for further investigations into dogs' 
sensitivity to human social conventions and its origins.
